Music Theory

Coloring the Same 12 Tones

Modes, the blues scale, and chord progressions, explained in plain language.

Before we start

Key takeaways

  • Modes are rotations of one major scale: same seven pitches, different home note.
  • Lydian raises the fourth (dreamy); Mixolydian lowers the seventh (bluesy, resolved).
  • There are 12 pitches per octave; enharmonic names like C♯ / D♭ depend on context.
  • The blues scale and the major-minor approach are practical hybrids, not textbook modes.
  • Chord numbers are scale degrees: a 2-5-1 in C is D minor → G7 → C major.
  • Stacked thirds follow the harmonic series; the dominant seventh pulls toward 1, but resolving is optional.
Modes · Lydian

Lydian: a major scale with a raised fourth

  • Take a major scale and raise the fourth degree a half step.
  • It's the fourth mode of the major scale.
  • That raised fourth is the color note: it gives Lydian its floating, dreamy quality.
  • Classic example: “Maria” from West Side Story.
Modes · Lydian

Two ways to build it

Start on the 4th

C major → F Lydian

Play C major starting from its fourth degree: F–G–A–B–C–D–E.

Raise the 4th

F major → F Lydian

Take F major and raise its fourth: B♭ becomes B.

Modes · Mixolydian

Mixolydian: major with a lowered seventh

  • The fifth mode of the major scale.
  • White keys from G: G–A–B–C–D–E–F.
  • The sound: bluesy and resolved rather than tense.

Chromatic notes

Twelve pitches, sometimes two names

  • The notes between the white keys complete the chromatic scale: 12 pitches per octave.
  • Each pitch can have two names depending on context, such as C♯ / D♭. These are enharmonic equivalents.
Chromatic notes

Any of the 12 notes can be home

  • Modes can be built on any of the 12 roots.
  • Black keys don't create new types of mode, only new tonal centers.

Blues

The blues scale: a practical hybrid

Major pentatonic, plus the minor third and the flat seventh.

GB♭CC♯DEF

In G blues, the C♯ is the “blue note.”

Blues

The major-minor collision

  • Combine the full G major and G minor scales and you get 11 of the 12 chromatic notes. Only A♭ is missing.
  • Players call this playing “outside” or the major-minor approach.
  • There's no single official name for it.
  • It's closer to real blues improvisation than any one named scale.

Harmony

Why stack thirds?

The major scale's spacing is uneven:

wholewholehalfwholewholewholehalf
  • That unevenness is why stacking thirds produces different chord qualities on different degrees.
  • Root, third, and fifth are the strongest partials in the harmonic series after the octave, so Western harmony defaulted to them.
  • Stack fourths or fifths instead and you get sus and power chords.
Harmony

The dominant seventh's pull

  • The flat seventh on the 5 chord creates the strong pull toward 1.
  • You can alter it further: ♭9, ♯9, 13.
  • But the root and third must stay a major third apart for it to work as a dominant.
Harmony

Resolution is optional

Resolving isn't a rule. It's the ear getting what it predicted.

Leave it unresolved and you get tension, the way film scoring and Debussy use it.
